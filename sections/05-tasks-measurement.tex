\section{Tasks and Measurement}
\label{sec:measurement}

\subsection{Deliverables with forced values}

Every task is a hidden-profile artifact. The facts required to complete a deliverable
are split across specialist counterparts; plausible-but-wrong \emph{distractors} are
planted alongside them, each one placed on a counterpart other than the holder of the
value it contradicts, and at least one inside the bounded roster so that no
configuration is immune by construction. Ground truth is designed rather than
inferred, so scoring is set arithmetic.

Two properties of the deliverable schema do the real work. First, every required
field is \emph{forced}: its value is determined by the facts, so a field that is a
matter of taste cannot appear. Second, the schema has two projections over one fact
set---a flat \emph{report} whose fields are values some counterpart holds, and a
\emph{chained decision} whose fields depend on one another. The same facts and the
same allocation serve both, which makes coordination depth a real dimension rather
than an artificial chain welded onto a flat task.

Elimination is what forces pooling. One constraint rules out one option, a second
rules out another, a third rules out a third, and each lives on a different
counterpart; the surviving choice is unreachable from any single one of them. Tasks
also carry a \emph{craft} component---a rendered artifact with structural
requirements---because a value can be retrieved in one message while a working
artifact cannot, which is where iteration and delegation depth begin to matter.

Three rules are fixed before any key is written. The schema---field names, types, and
the full enumeration vocabulary---is given to the agent, so that only values are
unknown and we are not measuring whether a model guesses our vocabulary.
Normalization is specified in advance: numbers parsed and compared exactly, dates
ISO-8601, strings casefolded and trimmed, enumerations restricted to the supplied
list, lists compared as sets. Evidence is matched on values rather than on internal
identifiers, which agents never see.

\metric{realization} deserves emphasis, because it is what keeps the study honest.
The allocation fixes how much any configuration \emph{could} obtain; dividing it out
leaves what the configuration actually converted---whether it located the right
counterparts, asked enough of them, and resolved the conflicts. The ceiling is
designed; the realization is not.

Two instrumentation rules follow from a defect we want to make impossible. Completion
is decided by the harness's deterministic checker and never read from a
condition-specific field: such a field exists on the private path of the deployed
system we draw from and has no public counterpart, so using it would let ``private
completes more'' prove itself. Token accounting is counted in the harness for the
same reason.

\subsection{Judged metrics, and the conditions that make them admissible}
\label{sec:judged}

Three quantities---overall utility, novelty, and diversity of approach---cannot be
recovered from an answer key without redefining them into something else. We measure
them, and we constrain how.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Pairwise, not absolute.} Deliverables are compared two at a time. A
      0--10 absolute scale drifts across a sweep and rewards length.
\item \textbf{Cross-family judge.} Outputs produced by one model family are judged by
      another. Same-family judging carries a documented self-preference bias, and our
      configurations differ systematically in output style---one cites more sources,
      another is more internally consistent---so a same-family judge would be at risk
      of scoring style.
\item \textbf{Blind to condition.} Condition labels and any configuration-identifying
      formatting are stripped before judging.
\item \textbf{Agreement reported.} A second judge scores a stratified subsample; we
      report agreement per metric and treat a low value as a limit on that metric, not
      as noise to average away.
\end{enumerate}

Keeping \metric{task\_utility} alongside \metric{requirement\_f1} buys a result for
free: the two measure overlapping things by different means, so their
\emph{disagreement} is itself reportable. If automatic keys and a blind judge diverge
on a nontrivial fraction of episodes, and divergence is directional, that is a
methodological finding for a field that increasingly uses one or the other alone.

\subsection{Honesty statement}

The allocation fixes how much each configuration can reach, so the \emph{existence}
of a task-dependent crossing is a property of the design and not a discovery. What is
measured is where the crossing falls, what it costs, and how much of the attainable
each configuration realizes. The seeded conditions and the access axis carry no such
guarantee, and they are the study's primary empirical content.
