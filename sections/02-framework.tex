\section{Three places a relationship can live}
\label{sec:framework}

Take the network as the unit of analysis: agents are nodes, and a request from one to
another is an edge. Relational structure then sits in exactly three places, and only
the first two are things a coordination layer checks.

\paragraph{Edge formation.} Which edges a requester may create: the scope of the
directory it can enumerate and address. This is the axis the word ``public'' most often
refers to, and the one broadcast networks for agents are built around---an agent
publishes what it needs and what it offers, and the network routes to whoever is
relevant \cite{eigenflux}.

\paragraph{Edge semantics.} What an existing edge carries. A counterpart may accept a
task and return a result while revealing nothing else, or expose a store to be listed
and read. The distinction is not ours: it separates agent-to-agent task protocols,
which deliberately keep a counterpart opaque \cite{a2a2025}, from granted resource
scopes, in which one principal hands another read access to files. A capability kernel
expresses both as capabilities over different namespaces, so a condition is a set of
grants rather than a different codebase.

\paragraph{Edge attributes.} What is asserted \emph{about} an edge: a reputation
score, a stated shared objective, a record of prior collaboration, a claim that answers
are logged and attributed. These are the field's most common steering lever, and they
rest on an assumption---that an agent builds some representation of its counterpart
from what it is told and acts differently because of it. Nothing in a runtime checks
them.

\subsection{Why the grouping is the contribution}

Nine dimensions are not nine degrees of freedom. In a deployed system they are heavily
coupled: an open directory tends to come with weak identity, which comes with diffuse
accountability and low exit cost. A study that compares an open network to a bounded
one moves all nine and reports one number, then attributes it to whichever dimension
the authors find most interesting.

The grouping breaks the coupling in the one way a controlled study can. Formation and
semantics are properties of the interaction layer: independently settable, checked on
every call, and not open to argument by a model. Attributes are properties of the
people behind the agents; a harness cannot instantiate them, only \emph{assert} them.

That is the experiment, not a limitation to apologize for: deployed networks communicate
trust, alignment, attribution and tenure as text on cards, so asking whether a model acts
on that text asks whether a large part of agent-network design does anything.

Only the first axis has an established literature---which links form, and at what price,
is strategic network formation \cite{jackson1996}. What an edge \emph{carries} has no such
body of work, and we take that distinction from deployed protocol design; attributes on an
edge are a standard attributed graph, but treating them as a control surface and measuring
whether they work is, to our knowledge, new.
