\section{Two regimes, nine dimensions}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Definition}

A \textbf{public} agent network is one in which interaction does not require a
pre-existing relationship: participation is open or nearly so, counterparts are
found by relevance, and an encounter is not presumed to repeat. A \textbf{private}
agent network is membership-bounded and its relationships predate the task: the
agents act for people who are already colleagues, and interaction is expected to
continue. A \textbf{hybrid} regime is neither a blend nor a recruitment funnel; it is
a single task that \emph{switches regime by phase}---a private core states the
problem, sanitizes it, consults a public periphery to widen the possibility space,
then returns to the private side to narrow the decision space, commit, and execute.
Public expands what is possible; private is where something is promised.

Table~\ref{tab:dims} states the nine dimensions along which the two regimes differ.
The first column is not new; versions of it appear throughout the literature on ties,
teams, and open innovation. What is new is the partition.

\subsection{Why the partition is the contribution}

Nine dimensions are not nine degrees of freedom. In any deployed system they are
heavily coupled: open membership tends to come with weak identity, which tends to
come with diffuse accountability and low exit cost. Presenting them as a flat list
implies an independence that does not exist and invites a study that varies all of
them at once and attributes the result to whichever one the authors find most
interesting.

The partition breaks the coupling in the one way a controlled study can. The
\arch{architectural} four are properties of the coordination layer. They can be set
independently, they are checked on every call, and a model has no way to circumvent
them. The \txt{textual} five are properties of the people behind the agents. A
harness cannot instantiate them; it can only \emph{assert} them---which is precisely
what a real agent card, reputation field, or relationship-history summary does.

That is not a limitation to apologize for. It is the experiment. Deployed agent
networks already communicate trust, alignment, accountability and tenure as text, on
cards and in context windows. Asking whether a model acts on that text is asking
whether a large and growing part of agent-network design does anything.

\subsection{The claim}

We predict a large asymmetry: the architectural group moves outcomes, the textual
group moves them little, and the gap between them is larger than the design
literature would suggest. Two consequences follow if this holds. First, comparisons
of ``public'' against ``private'' networks that do not equalize architecture are
measuring membership, not relationship. Second, effort spent enriching relational
metadata is misallocated relative to effort spent making the runtime enforce
something.

Both outcomes are publishable. If seeded trust does reduce verification cost and
seeded accountability does reduce error, that is a positive result with an immediate
design implication---relational metadata works, and here is the elasticity. Our
prediction is the reverse, and we state it in advance so that it can fail.
