\section{Introduction}

Two pictures of an agent network are in circulation. In the first, agents discover each
other in the open: they publish what they can do, broadcast what they need, and are
matched by relevance rather than acquaintance. In the second, agents act for people who
already work together: membership is bounded, interaction repeats, context accumulates.
The first is called public and the second private, and the difference is usually
described as one of visibility.

Visibility is the wrong frame. Twitter and WhatsApp do not differ mainly in who can be
seen; they differ in what it costs to open an edge and what happens after one is abused.
On the first, an account addresses any other, relevance is a claim in a profile, and a
bad actor changes handles. On the second, a conversation exists because a number was
already held or someone made an introduction, and misbehaviour costs the tie and
embarrasses whoever vouched. Reach, price, exposure and accuracy all follow from that.

Taking the edge as the primitive exposes three things that vary together and are reported
as one. \arch{Edge formation} is which edges a requester may create at all. \arch{Edge
semantics} is what an existing edge carries: a delegated task returning a result, or
direct access to state. \txt{Edge attributes} are the beliefs attached to an edge---a
reputation score, a stated shared objective, a note that the parties have worked together
before---which change nothing a runtime checks. Each regime supplies a characteristic
value on each axis, and a study that compares an open network to a closed one moves all
three and attributes the difference to whichever the authors find most interesting.

\paragraph{This paper separates them and prices them.} We define the regimes on the edge
(\S\ref{sec:framework}), give an instrument that sets the three axes independently and a
metric set that charges each one in accuracy, tokens and disclosure
(\S\ref{sec:quantifying}), and state the design question the decomposition makes
answerable: not which regime wins, but which configuration a setting should hold, and
whether the axis weights are constants of the framework or of the task
(\S\ref{sec:combination}). The coordination kernel is an instrument, not a contribution.

What we have measured so far is one task family, and it is summarised in
\S\ref{sec:evidence} with the full record---including the nulls and one
retraction---in the appendix. Two of its results were not designed and are the reason we
think the framework earns its keep. A bounded roster is shown far less misinformation
than an open directory and absorbs more of it, fabricating values 58 times against once,
because the filter that keeps bad claims out also removes the second source that would
catch them. And a single brokered hop---one roster member forwarding a request---recovers
a substantial part of the gap, while every assertion \emph{about} trust stays flat. An
introduction changes the graph; an endorsement is text on an edge that already exists.
The framework predicts that only the first can do work, and that is how it came out.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{A definition of public and private agent networks on the edge}, from which
      reach, cost and exposure follow, together with the three-axis decomposition it
      implies and what each regime supplies on each axis (\S\ref{sec:framework}).
\item \textbf{An instrument that sets the three axes independently}---one model, one
      runtime, one kernel, conditions as capability grants---and a metric set that prices
      an axis in accuracy, tokens and disclosure (\S\ref{sec:quantifying}).
\item \textbf{The combination problem, stated so it can be answered}: an optimum is a
      funnel depth per setting, and the test of the framework is whether the axis weights
      survive a change of task family (\S\ref{sec:combination}).
\item \textbf{A first decomposition on one task family}, including a controlled test of
      relational metadata against a length-matched placebo, and two undesigned results
      that bear directly on the framework (\S\ref{sec:evidence}).
\end{enumerate}

We do not study how a stranger becomes a teammate, and we make no safety claim about
either regime. We state in advance that the \emph{existence} of a task-dependent crossing
between regimes follows from how we allocate facts and is not a discovery; what is
measured is where it falls and what it costs.
