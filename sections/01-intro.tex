\section{Introduction}

Two pictures of an agent network are in circulation. In the first, agents discover each
other in the open: they publish what they can do, broadcast what they need, and are
matched by relevance rather than acquaintance. In the second, agents act for people who
already work together: membership is bounded, interaction repeats, context accumulates.
The first is called public and the second private, and the difference is usually
described as one of visibility.

Visibility is the wrong frame, and so is treating the two as endpoints of one dial.
On Twitter an account addresses any other, relevance is a claim in a profile, and a bad
actor changes handles. On WhatsApp a conversation exists because a number was already held
or someone made an introduction, the tie carries files and history, and misbehaviour costs
the tie and embarrasses whoever vouched. The two differ in what it costs to open an edge,
in what the edge carries once open, and in what is asserted about the counterpart---three
things at once, not one.

Take the edge as the primitive and the three separate. \arch{Edge formation} is which edges a requester may create at all. \arch{Edge
semantics} is what an existing edge carries: a delegated task returning a result, or
direct access to state. \txt{Edge attributes} are the beliefs attached to an edge---a
reputation score, a stated shared objective, a record that the parties have worked together
before---which change nothing a runtime checks. Public and private are two \emph{bundles}
in this space, not two definitions and not two settings of one dial, so a study that
compares them swaps all three at once. Worse, the two ways of pairing the axes give
different answers on the same data---$0.181$ and $0.314$---so the reported size of the
``public advantage'' is set by a bundling choice, not by a measurement. \textbf{Public versus private is not the experimental
variable}; $do(F)$, $do(S)$ and $do(A)$ are.

\paragraph{This paper unbundles them and prices the components.} We place the two regimes
as corners of $(F,S,A)$ rather than as definitions (\S\ref{sec:framework}). We give an
instrument that sets the three axes independently, and a metric set that charges each one
in accuracy, tokens and disclosure (\S\ref{sec:quantifying}). And we state the design
question the decomposition makes answerable: not which regime wins, but which configuration
a setting should hold, and whether the axis weights are constants of the framework or of
the task (\S\ref{sec:combination}). The coordination kernel is an instrument, not a
contribution.

\paragraph{Contributions.} (i) \textbf{Public and private recast as two bundles in an edge
space} $e=(F,S,A)$ rather than as two definitions, which makes ``public versus private'' a
bundle swap rather than a variable (\S\ref{sec:framework}). (ii) \textbf{An instrument that
sets the three axes independently}---one model, one runtime, one kernel, conditions as
capability grants---with a metric set that prices an axis in accuracy, tokens and
disclosure rather than accuracy alone (\S\ref{sec:quantifying}). (iii) \textbf{A
configuration problem in place of a binary comparison}, whose first result is that the
space is smaller than it looks and whose sharpest untested prediction is that an edge's
position moves (\S\ref{sec:combination}). (iv) \textbf{A first decomposition on one task
family}, with a length-matched placebo on the attribute axis and two undesigned results
(\S\ref{sec:evidence}).

We do not study how a stranger becomes a teammate, and we make no safety claim about
either regime. We state in advance that the \emph{existence} of a task-dependent crossing
between regimes follows from how we allocate facts and is not a discovery; what is
measured is where it falls and what it costs.
