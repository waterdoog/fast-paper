Agent networks encode relationships in two very different places. Some structure is
written as \emph{text}---agent cards, reputation fields, stated shared goals, tenure,
trust labels---and reaches a model only as tokens. Other structure is \emph{enforced
by architecture}: who is addressable at all, whether a counterpart exposes an
interface or a readable store, what persists between encounters. The public/private
distinction is described in the first vocabulary and implemented in the second, and
the two are rarely separated. We ask which of them changes what agents do. We define
public and private agent networks over nine relational dimensions, partition them into
an architectural group that a capability kernel enforces and a textual group that
exists only as seeded context, and operationalize the architectural group as a
$2\times2$ factorial over a discovery axis and an access axis, run as configurations
of one kernel, one runtime, and one model. Deliverables are hidden-profile artifacts
whose required values are forced by facts distributed across specialists, so primary
metrics are exact-match and counter-based; three secondary metrics no answer key can
reach are judged blind and pairwise by a cross-family model with agreement reported.
We predict the architectural group dominates: equalizing reach removes most of the
measured gap, store access changes what is possible more than what is done, and
seeded trust, alignment, accountability and tenure move behavior far less than their
prominence in agent-network design implies.
